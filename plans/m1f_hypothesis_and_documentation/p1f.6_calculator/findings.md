**On E-3, from S1f.6.1's round 3** ([design.md § Round 3](design.md#round-3-2026-11-11-several-representations-at-once)):
a theory can avoid it. A rule whose premises share no nested value is never matched by a scan, and
`build/s1f.6/prototype/r3/k.ein` computes the five operations over nested numbers with such rules alone, each
call carrying a continuation tag: 4 000 nine-digit divisions in 2.2 s, where the theory of round 2 does not
finish 1 000 in 45 s (`python3 r3_table.py`, 2026-11-11). The same table shows E-3 on fixed-width numbers too:
`num9`'s division joins its state with the divisor's multiples on the divisor, 193 ms for 250 divisions and
8.1 s for 4 000. The row stays open: a join on a nested value is the natural way to write a rule.

**On E-3, from S1f.6.3** ([its T2 record](s1f.6.3_addition.md#t2-record-2026-11-11)): the scan is of a premise
with an **unbound slot**. A premise every slot of which is bound is answered by one interned lookup, whatever
its arguments are, a nested fact too: `(add ?x ?y)` as a second premise of a rule that has bound both costs one
candidate a firing, and `(operand-is (add-first ?x ?y) ?u)`, with `?u` free, walks the relation, 681, 2 181 and
8 181 candidates a request at 250, 1 000 and 4 000 requests (`cargo test -p ein-infer --features counters
--test arith_batch -- --nocapture` on each spelling, 2026-11-11). `std.arith` uses the first once, on purpose:
`operand-name` asks `(named ?x)` before its `let`, so a number operand never walks the `let` facts. The rule of
the theory is unchanged, one premise that holds numbers.
