## What it asks

*"induction"* (the user, 2026-11-11). To say *x + y = y + x for every x and y* and mean every one. The
calculator and S1g.5.1 derive each true ground equation they are asked for and can state no universal
sentence. The stage sets out the ways to get further, tries each, and takes one theorem as far as it goes.

## Four ways to hold a universal statement, in rising strength

**1. Check every instance up to a bound.** Generate the requests, compute both sides, claim that they agree,
as [S1g.5.2](s1g.5.2_agreement_with_the_calculator.md)'s agreement programs do. It is a proof for the bound
and evidence beyond it. `ein oracle` is not needed: nothing is searched. This exists once S1g.5.1 does, and
is the baseline every stronger way is compared with.

**2. Search for a counterexample.** The numbers up to the bound as hypotheses, a rule that makes a state in
which the equation *holds* dead, or one that owes a counterexample: R is the counterexamples, and empty R is
way 1's result reached by the lattice. Worth one program, to say why it is the wrong tool here (nothing is
unknown; the lattice enumerates what a generator writes down) and where it becomes the right one: an
equation with an unknown, S1f.6.10's item 2.

**3. Check a written induction proof.** The object level. The statement is a formula with a variable,
`(All x (Eq (plus zero x) x))`, in S1g.4.3's representation, where `plus` and `zero` are now object-language
function symbols and `Eq` the object language's equality (a predicate of the theory, with its axioms:
reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, congruence under `s` and `plus`). Induction is one more rule of
S1g.4.4's checker:

> from `F[zero/x]`, and `(All x (Imp F F[(s x)/x]))`, conclude `(All x F)`.

It is a **schema over formulas**, recognised the way S1g.3.1 recognises an axiom: the line states F and x,
two substitution requests compute `F[zero/x]` and `F[(s x)/x]`, and the checker looks for the two cited
lines to be exactly those. Nothing else is new. Expected nearly possible: every ingredient is P1g.4's.
The proof itself is written by a person or generated by the Rust harness, and its lines below the induction
are equational reasoning from the recursion equations, which are the theory's axioms as formulas.

**4. Find the proof.** For an equation between terms, the induction's base and step are again equations, and
each can be attempted by *rewriting both sides with the recursion equations to a normal form and comparing*,
which is a demand-driven computation on terms **with a variable in them**: S1g.5.1's rules applied to
symbolic terms. `(plus x zero)` rewrites to `x`; `(plus zero x)` is stuck, which is exactly why that one
needs induction. So a small tactic is: try rewriting; if stuck, induct on a variable, and try rewriting the
base and the step using the hypothesis as one more rewrite rule. The hypothesis as a rewrite rule is **a
rule that exists only inside the step's sub-proof**: at the object level, a fact `(rewrites L R)` scoped by
the request's tag; at the meta level it would be a rule added under a hypothesis, which is rules as data
([F5](../../followups/f5_rules_as_data.md)). **To be tried** at the object level on `0 + n = n` and on
associativity; commutativity needs two lemmas, and whether the tactic finds them is not expected.

## The theorem

One, taken through ways 1, 3 and (attempted) 4, with every artefact:

- **`0 + n = n`**: the smallest theorem whose proof needs induction, since the recursion is on the other
  argument. Then, if it went through:
- **`S(m) + n = S(m + n)`**, and from both, **`m + n = n + m`**: the textbook's first real induction, with
  its two lemmas.

For each: the bounded check and its bound; the written proof, as data, checked, with the count of lines and
of checker firings; the corruption sweep (a wrong base case, a step that uses the conclusion in place of the
hypothesis: the classic circular proof, which the checker must reject); and what the tactic did.

## What each way licenses one to say

The page's closing table, in the vocabulary of
[S1g.1.2](../p1g.1_survey_and_design/s1g.1.2_two_levels.md) and against
[`standard_of_proof.md`](../../../docs/kernel/standard_of_proof.md):

| way | what was established | by whom | what it rests on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | the instances up to the bound | Ein's saturation | the theory's rules, and the generator |
| 3 | the theorem, in the object theory | a proof written outside; **checked** by Ein | the checker's rules being sound ([S1g.3.4](../p1g.3_proof_systems/s1g.3.4_soundness_and_completeness.md) and S1g.4.4's sampled check), and the induction rule itself, which is an axiom |
| 4 | the same | found by a theory written in Ein, checked as in 3 | the same |

And one sentence on what none of them is: a proof that the **meta-level** rules of S1g.5.1 compute a
commutative function. The object-level `plus` and the Ein rules `plus-zero` and `plus-succ` are two things;
that they correspond is checked by way 1, up to a bound, and argued by reading. Closing that gap would need
Ein's own rules as data.

## What it may find

- **That equational reasoning as checked lines is unbearably long** (every step a congruence and a
  transitivity). Then the checker gets one derived rule, *rewrite by an equation at a position*, and the
  page says the proofs are in a calculus with that rule.
- **That the tactic's scoped hypothesis is the cleanest witness for rules as data** the milestone has. It
  goes to [S1g.6.5](../p1g.6_engine_requirements/s1g.6.5_rules_as_data_and_refutations.md).
- **That way 2 is not pointless:** an obligation that owes a counterexample may be the plainest program for
  *is this conjecture false below the bound*. Reported either way.
